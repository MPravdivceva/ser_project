Datasets

The quality of a Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) system depends on the dataset it is trained on. A well-chosen dataset helps the model understand emotions accurately, making it more reliable in real-life situations. A good dataset should include diverse voices, natural emotional expressions, and high-quality recordings. Some datasets use acted emotions, while others capture real-life speech. Both have advantages, but models trained only on acted data may struggle to recognize emotions in everyday conversations. In practical applications, SER can be used in mental health support, smart assistants, and even car safety systems that detect driver fatigue. Choosing the right dataset ensures that these systems truly understand human emotions, making them more helpful and reliable in daily life.

Choosing the Right Dataset

A well-chosen dataset is crucial for a Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) system to accurately interpret human emotions in real-world situations. Some datasets focus on acted emotions, where professional actors simulate different moods, while others capture spontaneous, real-world speech, which can be more challenging but also more authentic.

By combining both types, an SER model can achieve better adaptability, improving applications like mental health support, customer interactions, and AI-driven assistants, making technology more intuitive and responsive.

CREMA-D Dataset

The CREMA-D (Crowd-Sourced Emotional Multimodal Actors Dataset) contains 7,442 audio-visual recordings from 91 professional actors (48 male, 43 female), covering six emotional categories: Anger, Disgust, Fear, Happiness, Neutral, and Sadness. Each sample is recorded with different emotional tones and is annotated through crowd-sourced human evaluations, making it a valuable resource for training multimodal emotion recognition models.

IEMOCAP Dataset

The IEMOCAP (Interactive Emotional Dyadic Motion Capture Database) consists of approximately 12 hours of audio-visual recordings from 10 professional actors (5 male, 5 female), engaging in both scripted and spontaneous conversations.

It includes multiple emotion categories such as Neutral, Happiness, Sadness, Anger, Surprise, Fear, and Frustration. Each utterance is carefully labeled using a combination of actor self-assessment and third-party annotations, making it a widely used dataset for both speech and multimodal emotion recognition research.
